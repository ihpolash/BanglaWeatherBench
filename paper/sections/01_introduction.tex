\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A time-series foundation model is pretrained once on a large heterogeneous corpus, then asked to forecast an
unseen series with no fitting, no feature engineering and no local expertise. If that promise holds where
forecasting capacity is scarcest, it matters. Those are the places with the fewest stations, the fewest trained
forecasters and the least compute, and they are often where a week of warning carries the largest consequences.
A model that runs zero-shot on a single GPU is precisely the technology they lack.

Whether it holds there is an empirical question current benchmarks do not answer, because the evidence base is
skewed in two ways at once. Geographically, general time-series suites treat weather as one domain among many and
draw it overwhelmingly from mid-latitude sources \citep{godahewa2021monash,aksu2024gifteval,qiu2024tfb,li2025tsfmbench};
across seven widely used benchmark papers, the words ``monsoon'' and ``Bangladesh'' do not appear. Representationally,
the weather benchmarks that \emph{are} globally comprehensive score against reanalysis
\citep{rasp2024weatherbench2,nathaniel2024chaosbench}. Reanalysis provides a spatially and temporally consistent reconstruction of the atmosphere, but it can differ
from direct station observations, particularly where observational coverage is sparse. Observation-based
evaluation exists for numerical and AI weather-prediction models \citep{jin2024weatherreal,gupta2026mausam}, but the
corresponding question for zero-shot time-series models has not been asked.

Bangladesh makes the question concrete. Its weather is governed by the South Asian summer monsoon, whose defining
feature, the alternation of active and break spells inside the rainy season, has no temperate analogue. The 35-station
record we build on shows the range. Median July rainfall reaches \SI{457}{\milli\metre} per station-month, against
\SI{0}{\milli\metre} in January. \SI{67.6}{\percent} of station-days are dry, yet the wet-day 99th percentile
is \SI{143}{\milli\metre}. The 2016--2023 test period alone contains 432 cyclone station-days across 14 storms. The
BMD record is dense, but the public international record is thin. Over the same period the ten Bangladeshi stations
in GHCN-Daily are about \SI{65}{\percent} complete, with 2021 almost entirely absent. The European stations we use as a
reference are \SI{99}{\percent} complete. That asymmetry is not only a property of some pretraining corpus: it sits inside
the model's input window at forecast time.

The question this paper asks is therefore simple to state. When zero-shot foundation models are scored against
what stations in a tropical monsoon climate actually recorded, do they still beat conventional forecasting
methods? Three narrower questions follow. Does scoring against reanalysis instead of stations make the models look
better than they are? If they do worse in Bangladesh than in Europe, is the cause the climate or the gaps in the
station record? And does an aggregate score hide failures in particular monsoon regimes, such as the active spells
that bring the heaviest rain?

We introduce \textbf{BanglaWeatherBench}, a benchmark designed so that its headline number is not the only thing it
produces. It defines 16 daily forecasting tasks over 170{,}571 rolling-origin windows: eight score forecasts against
station observations and eight against reanalysis. The
forecast target is 35 Bangladesh Meteorological Department stations; beside them sit 10 co-located GHCN-Daily
stations, 32 European GHCN-Daily stations as a temperate reference, and NASA POWER reanalysis at all 77 station
coordinates. A seventeenth task covers CHIRPS dekadal rainfall over 64 districts, from an independent source.
Scoring is stratified by monsoon phase and by active/break spell.

A worse score in Bangladesh than in Europe could come from the climate, from a different observing network, from
the scoring target or from missing data. Three controls separate these. The European stations come from
GHCN-Daily, the same network and quality control as the ten Bangladeshi stations they are compared with, so a
difference between the two is not a difference of network. The reanalysis track sits at the same coordinates as
the stations, so comparing the two isolates the scoring target. Gap-matched contexts copy Bangladesh's
missing-data patterns onto the European stations' inputs, for the trained baselines as well as the foundation
models. A deficit that survives them is therefore not about missing data.

Two commitments shape what we are willing to claim. The first is significance. Forecast windows overlap heavily,
so we treat the forecast origin, not the window, as the unit of resampling. The primary statistical analysis
consists of 48 comparisons. For each of the 16 tasks and each of the leads 1, 7 and 30 days, we choose the best
foundation model and the best baseline on the 2011--2015 validation years. We then compare them on the
2016--2023 test years. These comparisons are corrected for multiplicity; every other interval in the paper is reported as unadjusted.
Many margins that look decisive as point estimates do not survive the primary test. The second is leakage: we audit each model's
pretraining corpus against its technical report. Leakage-free zero-shot evaluation is not itself novel
\citep{qiao2026time}; here it is a precondition for reading a geographic contrast as a geographic effect.

The benchmark's central contribution is an evaluation design that helps distinguish differences in model
performance associated with climate, observing network, scoring target and missing data. With it, a zero-shot score on tropical station data can be read for what it does and does not show. Evaluating 8 zero-shot foundation models against 10
trained and statistical baselines this way yields a mixed result, summarised in five findings.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Foundation models lead, but most of the lead is not separable.} With both comparators chosen on
    the validation years, the best model beats the best baseline in 17 of 48 Holm-corrected comparisons, loses 5
    and is indistinguishable in 26. On station observations it wins 5 of 24, three of them at a one-day lead,
    against 12 of 24 on reanalysis. The six leading models and PatchTST sit within one critical difference.
    Choosing the comparators on the test windows instead gives 14, 5 and 29; no verdict reverses.
  \item \textbf{Nothing beats climatology on tropical rainfall} beyond a one-day lead at the BMD stations, or at
    any horizon on dekadal rainfall, a concrete boundary on the ``forecast anything'' claim.
  \item \textbf{Reanalysis overstates skill} relative to the stations it represents. For rainfall, temperature and
    humidity, 67 of 81 unadjusted intervals favour reanalysis and 2 favour the stations; sunshine, compared against
    shortwave radiation, is mostly inconclusive.
  \item \textbf{The tropical temperature deficit is narrow, mostly about data, and rests on ten stations.} At a 30-day lead, 4 of 8 models
    lose more skill than a like-for-like control, with unadjusted intervals that exclude zero; about
    \SI{60}{\percent} of the raw penalty is explained by sparser context, and doubling the context does not remove
    the remainder.
  \item \textbf{Skill reverses by regime}: strong in break spells, worse than climatology in active spells; 61 of
    72 unadjusted intervals, blocked by spell event, exclude zero. The sample is 11 or 12 active and 18 to 21 break
    events, so this is a pattern to test elsewhere rather than an established operational failure.
\end{enumerate}

Finding 4 is easy to misreport. It does not support a blanket claim that foundation models fail in the Global
South. Stated correctly it is also the more actionable result: much of the deficit is a data-availability problem
that observation programmes and gap-tolerant architectures can address.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (i) A released, leakage-tested benchmark for a tropical-monsoon climate, with splits,
strata, a datasheet and per-source licensing. (ii) Controls rather than a leaderboard alone: a same-network
temperate reference, a reanalysis track at identical coordinates, and a gap-matched control applied to models and
baselines alike. (iii) A significance protocol matched to overlapping rolling-origin panels, with one primary
family and every other interval labelled as unadjusted.
(iv) To our knowledge the first evaluation of current zero-shot time-series foundation models on South Asian
station observations including precipitation, stratified by monsoon phase, with a pretraining-overlap audit.
(v) Reproducibility at the compute scale of the places the benchmark is about: 4.16 GPU-hours on a Tesla T4.
